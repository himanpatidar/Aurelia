Sterling silver vs gold-plated vs steel: which should you buy?

Four materials, four very different lifespans and price points. A straight comparison of how each one behaves on real skin over real years.

Share

Four materials cover almost all jewelry under ₹10,000: sterling silver, gold-plated brass, stainless steel and rose-gold plating. They look more similar in a photograph than they behave on a wrist. Here is how each one actually performs.

925 sterling silver

Sterling silver is 92.5% silver with 7.5% other metals, usually copper, added for hardness — pure silver is too soft to hold a setting. It is a genuinely precious metal, it takes a high polish, and it has a cool white brightness that plating imitates but does not quite match.

Its weakness is the one everybody knows: it tarnishes. Untreated, in a humid Indian climate and worn daily, expect visible dulling in two to six weeks. The copper in the alloy makes this slightly worse than pure silver would be.

Sealed with rhodium, the same metal behaves completely differently — twelve to twenty-four months of brightness. So "silver" as a category tells you almost nothing; what matters is whether it has been treated.

Buy it for: pieces where the metal is the point — a solitaire ring, a fine tennis bracelet, anything you want to read as fine jewelry. Expect to pay: mid-range. Can be re-plated: yes, indefinitely.

18K gold-plated brass

A brass core with a thin layer of real gold on the outside. The gold is genuine; there is just not very much of it. Quality varies more here than in any other category, and the variable is thickness measured in microns.

Under one micron is what you find in very cheap jewelry and it is decorative only — weeks of wear, not months. One and a half microns, hardened and sealed, is what we use, and it holds for twelve to eighteen months of daily wear. Solid gold, for reference, is effectively permanent, and costs perhaps fifteen times as much.

What wears gold plating is friction, not water. Stacked rings grinding against each other, a bracelet catching a desk edge, a chain rubbing under a collar. Rotate your pieces and the same plating lasts years.

The second thing to check is whether the piece was plated before or after assembly. Plating flat components and then joining them leaves every jump ring and joint bare — and those are the first points to discolour. It is why our charm bracelet has its rings soldered fully closed before it goes in the bath.

Buy it for: warm gold tone at an accessible price, statement pieces, layered necklaces. Expect to pay: low to mid. Can be re-plated: yes.

316L surgical stainless steel

The dark horse, and our recommendation for anything you intend to never take off. 316L is the marine-grade alloy used for surgical implants. Its corrosion resistance is not a coating — it is a property of the metal itself, which forms a self-repairing passive oxide layer. Scratch it and the protection reforms.

It is nickel-bearing but in a bound, non-leaching form, which is why it is the standard for surgical implants and why it suits reactive skin better than most "hypoallergenic" plated jewelry.

Add a PVD gold coating — molecularly bonded rather than layered on — and you get gold colour with steel durability. Chlorine, seawater, sunscreen, sweat, sleeping in it: none of it matters. This is why our steel pieces carry a 24-month guarantee and our unplated brushed cuff carries a lifetime one.

The tradeoffs are real, though. Steel is hard, which makes it difficult to resize and impossible to re-plate. It is also denser and slightly heavier than brass. And it does not take fine detail as well as silver, so you see it used for hoops, chains and cuffs rather than intricate settings.

Buy it for: everyday hoops, chains, anything worn in water. Expect to pay: mid. Can be re-plated: no — but rarely needs it.

Rose gold plating

Rose gold is not a separate metal — it is gold alloyed with copper, and the copper is what gives it the warm pink tone. As a plating it behaves like yellow gold plating with one extra consideration: the copper content means acidic skin chemistry can shift the tone slightly over a long period, usually towards a deeper pink.

That is not damage and it is not tarnish, but it does mean rose gold benefits more than most finishes from being wiped after wear. On a sealed piece the effect is very slow; on unsealed rose gold plating it can be noticeable within months.

Buy it for: warmth against cooler skin tones, pieces meant to look softer than yellow gold. Expect to pay: low to mid. Can be re-plated: yes.

So which should you buy?

The honest answer depends on one question: will you take it off?

If you never take it off — the hoops you sleep in, the chain you shower in — buy PVD steel. Nothing else at this price survives that treatment, and the guarantee reflects it.

If it is a piece you put on for the day and take off at night, rhodium-sealed sterling silver or sealed 18K gold-plated brass are both excellent, and you should choose on colour and price rather than durability. Both will look new in a year with minimal care.

If you are buying your first good piece, start with something under ₹2,500 that you will genuinely wear daily rather than something expensive you will save for occasions. Jewelry that lives in a box does not need anti-tarnish technology, and jewelry that lives on your body is where the technology earns its keep.
